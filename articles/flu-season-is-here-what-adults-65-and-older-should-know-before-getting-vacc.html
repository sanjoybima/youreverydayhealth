Flu Season Is Here: What Adults 65 and Older Should Know Before Getting Vaccinated

The CDC says flu vaccination remains especially important for adults 65 and older, who face a higher risk of serious complications.

The CDC's current guidance says people 65 and older are at higher risk of serious flu complications and preferentially recommends certain higher-dose or adjuvanted vaccines for that age group. The right choice still depends on age, health history and the vaccines available. A pharmacist or clinician can help you decide which option is appropriate. Why age matters

The CDC considers adults 65 and older to be at higher risk of serious flu complications. Aging can change the immune response, and chronic conditions such as heart or lung disease can make respiratory infections harder to tolerate. That is why vaccination and early attention to symptoms are especially important in this age group.

Which vaccine should you get?

CDC guidance gives preferential recommendations for certain higher-dose or adjuvanted flu vaccines for adults 65 and older. That does not mean one specific product is right for everyone. Availability varies, and a pharmacist or clinician can help you select an age-appropriate option based on what is available and your health history.

Vaccination is only one part of prevention

Hand hygiene, staying away from people who are sick when possible, improving indoor ventilation and seeking care promptly when symptoms are severe can also reduce risk. If you have a chronic condition, ask your clinician how flu illness could affect it and what warning signs should trigger a call.

What about timing?

Flu activity changes from season to season. The practical goal is to be vaccinated before influenza is widely circulating in your community, while recognizing that protection can be useful later in the season too. Your clinician can advise you if you are unsure about timing.

When to seek help

Older adults can sometimes have less obvious symptoms than younger people. Difficulty breathing, chest pain, confusion, severe weakness, dehydration or rapidly worsening illness should not be ignored. Emergency symptoms require emergency care rather than waiting for a routine appointment.

Questions to ask a clinician

Bring a short list rather than trying to remember everything in the appointment. Ask what the diagnosis or recommendation means, what the alternatives are, which symptoms require urgent attention, and when you should follow up.

The evidence question

Health headlines often turn an association into a promise. A study can show that two things occur together without proving that one causes the other. When reading health news, look for the size of the study, the population studied, whether it was peer reviewed, and whether other studies support the finding.

What the evidence can and cannot tell you

A respected health source may summarize a study, guideline or surveillance report, but the strength of the evidence depends on the underlying research. Observational studies can identify patterns without proving cause and effect. Clinical trials can answer some treatment questions more directly, while guidelines combine evidence with expert judgment and practical considerations. A headline should never be treated as stronger evidence than the study itself.

How to prepare for a medical conversation

Write down when a symptom started, what makes it better or worse, and any related changes in medication, sleep, appetite or activity. Bring the list to the appointment. This gives the clinician a clearer picture and makes the visit more useful than trying to remember everything in the moment.

A note about online health information

Search engines and social platforms are useful for finding questions to ask, but they are poor substitutes for individualized care. If two sources disagree, check whether they are discussing the same population, the same definition and the same outcome. Then use a clinician for the personal decision.

Bottom line

Good health information should leave you better informed, not frightened into action. Use this article to identify questions, then confirm personal recommendations with a qualified health professional.

A practical checklist

  • Write down the main question you want answered.
  • Bring a current medication and supplement list.
  • Ask what symptoms should trigger urgent care.
  • Check the date and source of health claims before acting.
  • Follow up when a clinician recommends it.
For readers 55+: The most useful next step is usually a small, verifiable action—not a rushed decision based on a headline. If the issue affects your health, benefits, taxes or legal rights, use the appropriate professional or official agency.
