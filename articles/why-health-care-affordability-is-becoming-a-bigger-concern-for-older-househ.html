Why Health-Care Affordability Is Becoming a Bigger Concern for Older Households

New research shows that paying for needed health care remains a major concern for Americans, including people who have coverage.

Gallup reported that fewer than half of Americans said they could afford needed care and medications, a five-year low in its 2026 polling. Separate Commonwealth Fund research found that older Americans on Medicare can still face higher out-of-pocket spending and cost-related access problems than older adults in peer countries. What to keep in mind Review your plan's annual out-of-pocket exposure. Ask about generic or lower-cost medication options. Use preventive services that your coverage includes. Keep a list of bills and explanation-of-benefits statements. Ask for written estimates before major non-emergency care when possible. A useful rule: Slow down when a headline asks you to act immediately. Verify the source, check the date and look for the original agency, research paper or provider information. The bigger picture Insurance rule

Why affordability matters even with insurance

Having health insurance does not mean every medical expense is predictable. Premiums, deductibles, coinsurance, prescription costs, dental and vision care and services outside a network can all add up. Gallup's 2026 polling found that fewer than half of Americans said they could afford needed care and medicines, a five-year low.

Older adults face a different cost mix

People over 55 may use health services more often and may manage multiple prescriptions or chronic conditions. Commonwealth Fund research has also highlighted how out-of-pocket costs can affect older adults with Medicare. That makes a health budget part of retirement planning rather than a separate issue.

What you can control

You cannot control every medical bill, but you can improve visibility. Keep an annual list of premiums, recurring prescriptions, deductibles and expected appointments. Ask whether a generic medicine or different pharmacy is appropriate, and use covered preventive services when available.

Ask before the bill arrives

For non-emergency care, ask the provider what service is planned and whether your insurance is expected to cover it. For larger procedures, ask about facility fees and out-of-network clinicians. A written estimate cannot guarantee the final bill, but it gives you something concrete to question.

Don't delay necessary care just because it is expensive

Cost concerns can lead people to postpone care. If you are unsure how to pay, ask the provider about financial assistance, payment plans or lower-cost alternatives. The right response to an unaffordable bill is to seek information, not simply ignore the medical need.

Questions to ask a clinician

Bring a short list rather than trying to remember everything in the appointment. Ask what the diagnosis or recommendation means, what the alternatives are, which symptoms require urgent attention, and when you should follow up.

The evidence question

Health headlines often turn an association into a promise. A study can show that two things occur together without proving that one causes the other. When reading health news, look for the size of the study, the population studied, whether it was peer reviewed, and whether other studies support the finding.

What the evidence can and cannot tell you

A respected health source may summarize a study, guideline or surveillance report, but the strength of the evidence depends on the underlying research. Observational studies can identify patterns without proving cause and effect. Clinical trials can answer some treatment questions more directly, while guidelines combine evidence with expert judgment and practical considerations. A headline should never be treated as stronger evidence than the study itself.

How to prepare for a medical conversation

Write down when a symptom started, what makes it better or worse, and any related changes in medication, sleep, appetite or activity. Bring the list to the appointment. This gives the clinician a clearer picture and makes the visit more useful than trying to remember everything in the moment.

A note about online health information

Search engines and social platforms are useful for finding questions to ask, but they are poor substitutes for individualized care. If two sources disagree, check whether they are discussing the same population, the same definition and the same outcome. Then use a clinician for the personal decision.

Bottom line

Good health information should leave you better informed, not frightened into action. Use this article to identify questions, then confirm personal recommendations with a qualified health professional.

A practical checklist

  • Write down the main question you want answered.
  • Bring a current medication and supplement list.
  • Ask what symptoms should trigger urgent care.
  • Check the date and source of health claims before acting.
  • Follow up when a clinician recommends it.
For readers 55+: The most useful next step is usually a small, verifiable action—not a rushed decision based on a headline. If the issue affects your health, benefits, taxes or legal rights, use the appropriate professional or official agency.
